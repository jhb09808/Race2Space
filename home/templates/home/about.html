About the project

A frontier that belongs to everyone.

Race2Space documents and celebrates the rise of developing nations in space — the agencies, missions and milestones of countries too often left out of the story of space exploration.

Why this exists

For most of the twentieth century, "the space race" meant two superpowers. That story is out of date. Today, more than thirty nations run space programs — and some of the most remarkable progress is coming from the Global South: from India landing near the Moon's south pole and the UAE reaching Mars, to Kenya, Egypt, Brazil, Indonesia and dozens of others building satellites and standing up national space agencies.

These programs rarely make headlines, yet their work is deeply practical — satellites that improve harvests, warn of floods, connect remote communities and monitor a changing climate. Race2Space exists to bring that overlooked story together in one place, and to show that space is no longer the preserve of a wealthy few but a shared human frontier.

About the data

Race2Space draws on public sources — including the United Nations Office for Outer Space Affairs (UNOOSA), national space-agency publications, and open data feeds for live launches and orbital tracking. Budget figures are indicative annual estimates compiled from government and industry reporting, not audited accounts, and some (such as China's) are best-available approximations. The aim is an honest, accessible overview, not a definitive financial record.

Who's behind it

Race2Space is an independent project by Jerome Bustarga. What began as a digital-humanities research project grew into a living platform dedicated to the countries reaching for space against the odds. It's built and maintained by one person, with the help of modern web and AI tools, and it keeps evolving.
